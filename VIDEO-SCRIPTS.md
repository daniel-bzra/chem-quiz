# Video scripts

18 videos, one per question. Target length **35–45 seconds each** →
18 × ~40 s ≈ **12 minutes total**, which matches the brief (10–12 min, 30–45 s per video).

**File names must match exactly**, otherwise the quiz shows a placeholder:

```
videos/q01.mp4 … videos/q18.mp4
```

Structure that works for every video (and scores well on
*"explains clearly and concisely why an answer is wrong"*):

| Seconds | What you say |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Name the trap by its **content**: *"If you thought boiling breaks the bonds inside the molecule…"* |
| 5–25 | The reasoning / the calculation, shown on paper or on screen |
| 25–35 | The correct answer and **why** it is correct |
| 35–40 | One sentence to remember |

> **Never say "option B" or "the second answer".** The quiz shuffles the four
> options on every run, so the letters are different for every user. Always
> refer to what an answer *says*, not where it sits.

Speak English (B2). Show the working — a phone camera pointed at a
sheet of paper is completely fine and often clearer than slides.

## C1 — Substances & properties

**q01 — Separating sand, salt and water**
> Three components, so you need two separation steps, and each step has to use a property where the components actually differ. Sand does not dissolve — that is a particle-size difference, so filtration removes it. The salt is dissolved, so it goes through the filter with the water. Now you have only two components left, and they differ in boiling point: let the water evaporate and the salt stays behind. The common mistake is evaporating first — then salt and sand are two dry powders in the same beaker, and a filter cannot separate two solids. Rule: always ask which property differs.

**q02 — Pure substance or mixture?**
> A pure substance boils at one fixed temperature. Put a thermometer in boiling water and it stays at 100 degrees until the last drop is gone. Now do the same with salt water: the temperature keeps climbing, because water leaves first and the solution gets more concentrated. That constant plateau is the test. Transparency proves nothing — vinegar is transparent. Density proves nothing — you can mix a solution to any density. And a filter only stops undissolved particles. Fixed melting and boiling points are the fingerprint of a pure substance.

## C2 — Structure of matter

**q03 — Rutherford's gold foil**
> Two observations, two conclusions. First: nearly all alpha particles go straight through a foil that is thousands of atoms thick. So the atom must be mostly empty space. Second: a tiny fraction bounces almost straight back. An alpha particle is heavy and positive — only something equally positive and far denser can turn it around. That is the nucleus: tiny, heavy, positive. Electrons cannot do it; they are seven thousand times lighter, like a ping-pong ball trying to stop a bowling ball. And a uniformly charged sphere would deflect everything a little and nothing a lot.

**q04 — Why sodium and potassium behave alike**
> Sodium has 11 electrons, potassium has 19 — completely different numbers. But look at the outer shell: both have exactly one. Chemistry happens in the outer shell, so both atoms do the same thing — they give that one electron away and become a singly charged positive ion. That is why they sit in the same column of the periodic table and why both react violently with water. Different protons, different mass, same valence electrons, same chemistry. A group is a family because of the outer shell.

## C3 — Chemical bonding

**q05 — Why molten salt conducts**
> A current needs charge carriers that can move. In sodium chloride the charge carriers are already there — it is built from sodium ions and chloride ions. But in the solid crystal every ion is locked into its place in the lattice, so nothing flows. Melting does not create the ions and does not create electrons; it just breaks the lattice apart so the ions can drift. Same thing happens when you dissolve it in water. Ions present but frozen means no current; ions free to move means current.

**q06 — Why nitrogen forms exactly NH₃**
> Nitrogen sits in group 5, so it brings 5 valence electrons. The octet rule says it wants 8. Eight minus five is three, so it needs three more shared electrons, which means three bonds — and the two electrons it did not use stay behind as a lone pair. That gives you NH₃ with three N–H bonds and one lone pair on top, which is also why ammonia is pyramidal, not flat. Hydrogen always forms one bond, because it has one electron and needs two. Count the valence electrons, fill to eight, and the formula falls out.

## C4 — Amount of substance

**q07 — Mass of 500 L of ammonia**
> Never jump from litres to grams. Go through moles. At standard conditions one mole of any gas takes 22.4 litres, so 500 divided by 22.4 gives 22.3 moles. Check the units — litres divided by litres per mole leaves moles. Now the molar mass: ammonia is 14 plus three times 1, so 17 grams per mole. 22.3 times 17 is about 380 grams. If you got 8500 you multiplied 500 by 17 and skipped the molar volume entirely; if you got 22 you stopped at the number of moles.

**q08 — Concentration of a NaOH solution**
> Concentration is moles per litre, so both parts need converting. First the mass: 4.0 grams divided by 40.0 grams per mole gives 0.10 moles. Then the volume: 500 millilitres is 0.500 litres — that decimal point is where most marks are lost. Now divide: 0.10 divided by 0.500 is 0.20 moles per litre. Sanity check: the same amount in one full litre would be 0.10, and half the volume means double the concentration, so 0.20 is right.

## C5 — Chemical reactions

**q09 — The oxyhydrogen (Knallgas) reaction**
> Balance first: two H₂ plus O₂ gives two H₂O. Now convert both volumes to moles: 16.8 over 22.4 is 0.750 moles of hydrogen, 8.4 over 22.4 is 0.375 moles of oxygen. Their ratio is exactly two to one, which is what the equation asks for, so neither is left over. The equation also says you get as many water molecules as hydrogen molecules, so 0.750 moles of water — times 18 grams per mole gives 13.5 grams. Check with mass conservation: 1.5 grams of hydrogen plus 12 grams of oxygen is 13.5. It fits.

**q10 — What a catalyst actually does**
> Draw the energy diagram. Reactants on the left, products on the right, and a hill in between — the activation energy. A catalyst does one thing: it lowers that hill by offering a different route. More particles now have enough energy to get over it, so the reaction runs faster, or runs at a lower temperature. What it does **not** do is move the left and right ends of the diagram. The energy released stays exactly the same, and an endothermic reaction stays endothermic. And the catalyst comes out unchanged — that is why a tiny amount converts tonnes of material.

## C6 — Intermolecular forces

**q11 — Methanol boils higher than chloromethane**
> Boiling never breaks the bonds inside a molecule — that is the key misconception here. Boiling only pulls molecules away from each other, so the only thing that matters is the force *between* them. Chloromethane is a dipole because C–Cl is polar, so it has dipole–dipole forces. Methanol has an O–H group, and hydrogen sitting directly on oxygen means hydrogen bonds — the strongest of the intermolecular forces. Those cost far more energy to overcome, so methanol boils 89 degrees higher even though it is the lighter molecule. See O–H or N–H, think hydrogen bonds.

**q12 — Why non-polar chlorine can be liquefied**
> Cl₂ is two identical atoms, so the electronegativity difference is zero and there is no permanent dipole. There is no hydrogen, so no hydrogen bonds. And there are no ions. So what holds it together at minus 35 degrees? The electrons are moving all the time, and for a split second they sit more on one side than the other. That makes a temporary dipole, which pulls on the electrons of the neighbour and induces one there too. That is a van der Waals force — weak, short-lived, but present between all particles. It is why even helium can be liquefied.

## C7 — Acids & bases

**q13 — pH 3 versus pH 6**
> The pH scale is logarithmic, and that is the whole question. pH 3 means the oxonium concentration is ten to the minus three moles per litre; pH 6 means ten to the minus six. Divide them and you get ten to the power three — a factor of one thousand. Subtracting 6 minus 3 gives you the number of steps, not the factor; each of those three steps is itself a factor of ten. And watch the direction: lower pH means more acidic and a higher H₃O⁺ concentration. One pH unit, factor ten. Always.

**q14 — Neutralisation volume**
> Never compare volumes directly — compare amounts of substance. The base: 0.0250 litres times 0.100 moles per litre gives 2.50 millimoles of sodium hydroxide. The equation is one to one, so you need exactly 2.50 millimoles of hydrochloric acid. Now go back to a volume: 2.50 millimoles divided by 0.200 moles per litre gives 12.5 millilitres. And it makes sense — the acid is twice as concentrated, so you need half the volume. Two clean steps, and check that the answer is plausible.

## C8 — Redox reactions

**q15 — Oxidation number of chromium in Cr₂O₇²⁻**
> Set up the little equation. Oxygen is almost always minus two, and there are seven of them, so that is minus fourteen. The whole ion carries a charge of minus two, and the oxidation numbers must add up to that charge. So two times x, minus fourteen, equals minus two. Two x is plus twelve, and x is plus six. Two things people get wrong: forgetting to divide by the two chromium atoms, which gives plus twelve, and confusing this with permanganate, where manganese is plus seven. Count the atoms, write the equation, divide.

**q16 — Zinc, copper and the electrochemical series**
> Only one of these two beakers does anything. Zinc is the less noble metal — it is higher in the electrochemical series and gives up its electrons more readily. So in the copper sulfate beaker, zinc hands two electrons to each Cu²⁺ ion: zinc dissolves as Zn²⁺, and copper deposits as a red-brown layer on the strip while the blue colour of the solution fades. In the other beaker nothing happens, because copper is more noble and will not give its electrons to zinc ions. The series tells you the direction, and it only runs one way.

## C9 — Organic chemistry

**q17 — Butane versus 2-methylpropane**
> Same molecular formula, C₄H₁₀, so the same molar mass — mass cannot be the answer. No oxygen, no nitrogen, so no hydrogen bonds either. What differs is the shape. Butane is a straight chain; the molecules lie against each other like pencils in a box and touch over a large surface, so many van der Waals contacts. 2-methylpropane is branched and compact, more like a ball, so neighbouring molecules touch over a smaller area and there are fewer contacts. Weaker total attraction means a lower boiling point. More branching, lower boiling point.

**q18 — How the body breaks ethanol down**
> Ethanol is a primary alcohol — the OH sits on a carbon that still carries hydrogen atoms. Oxidation of a primary alcohol always runs in two steps. Step one: the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase removes hydrogen and you get the aldehyde, ethanal. Step two: ethanal is oxidised further to the carboxylic acid, ethanoic acid, which the body then breaks down completely. The carbon skeleton never changes length — oxidation only changes the functional group. And ethanal is the important bit: it is far more toxic than ethanol and it is what gives you the hangover.
