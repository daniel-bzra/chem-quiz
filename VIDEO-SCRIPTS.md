# Video scripts

Word-for-word scripts for the 18 explanation videos, one per question.

**18 videos · about 39 s each · 11.9 minutes in total**
(the brief asks for 30–45 s per video and 10–12 minutes overall)

## How to use this

- **Read it out; do not learn it by heart.** It is written the way people speak.
  Nothing has to be added or explained in your own words — everything that needs
  saying is already there.
- **Copy the board block exactly as it stands.** Do not reword or shorten it; it
  matches the spoken text line for line.
- Where drawing steps are given, work through them in order and the right sketch
  comes out. Do this before you start filming — it is the real preparation.
- Each paragraph is one beat. Pause briefly between paragraphs.
- If a take runs past 45 seconds, drop the **last** paragraph. It is always the
  check or the summary, never the reasoning.
- The pronunciation line covers the awkward words. The capitalised syllable is
  the stressed one.

> **Never say "option B" or "the second answer".** The quiz shuffles the four
> options on every run, so the letters differ for every user. Always refer to
> what an answer *says*.

## C1 — Substances & properties

### q01 · Separating sand, salt and water

`q01.mp4` · ~39 s · 95 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
sand + salt + water

1) FILTER      ->  sand stays on the paper
2) EVAPORATE   ->  salt stays in the dish

wrong order: 2 dry powders, filter useless
```

**Pronunciation:** *filtrate* → FIL-trayt · *dissolve* → di-ZOLV

**Say**

> Three components, so you need two steps, and each step has to use a property where the components really differ.
>
> Sand does not dissolve. That is a difference in particle size, so filtration takes the sand out.
>
> The salt is dissolved, so it passes through the filter with the water.
>
> Two things are left, and they differ in boiling point. Let the water evaporate, and the salt stays behind.
>
> The trap is doing it the other way round. Evaporate first and you get two dry powders in one beaker, and a filter cannot separate those.


### q02 · Pure substance or mixture

`q02.mp4` · ~39 s · 95 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
PURE  :  boils at ONE fixed temperature
MIXTURE:  boils over a temperature RANGE

transparency  -> proves nothing
density       -> proves nothing
filter test   -> proves nothing
```

**And draw this**

1. Draw an L: a vertical line on the left, a horizontal line along the bottom.
2. Write "T (°C)" next to the vertical line and "time" under the horizontal one.
3. Draw a line that rises from the bottom left and then goes flat. Write "100 °C - pure water" on the flat part.
4. Draw a second line that rises and keeps rising slowly. Write "salt water" on it.

**Pronunciation:** *fingerprint* → FING-ger-print

**Say**

> Here is the test that actually works. Put a thermometer in the liquid and boil it.
>
> Pure water sits at one hundred degrees and stays there until the last drop is gone. A flat line.
>
> Now boil salt water. The temperature keeps climbing, because the water leaves first and the solution gets more concentrated.
>
> That difference is your evidence.
>
> Transparency proves nothing, vinegar is transparent. Density proves nothing, you can mix a solution to almost any density. And a filter only stops undissolved particles.
>
> A fixed boiling temperature is the fingerprint of a pure substance.


## C2 — Structure of matter

### q03 · The gold-foil experiment

`q03.mp4` · ~39 s · 95 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
straight through  ->  atom is mostly EMPTY
bounced back      ->  tiny, dense, POSITIVE nucleus

electron = 7000x lighter than an alpha particle
```

**And draw this**

1. Draw one vertical line down the middle of the sheet. Write "gold foil" next to it.
2. From the left, draw four straight arrows that pass right through the line. Write "most: straight through".
3. Draw one more arrow from the left that hits the line and turns back to the left. Write "a few: bounce back".
4. Put a fat dot on the line where that arrow turns, and write "nucleus" next to the dot.

**Pronunciation:** *nucleus* → NEW-klee-us · *alpha* → AL-fuh · *deflect* → di-FLEKT

**Say**

> Two observations, and each one gives you a conclusion.
>
> First: almost every alpha particle goes straight through a foil thousands of atoms thick. So the atom is mostly empty space.
>
> Second: a few bounce almost straight back. An alpha particle is heavy and positive, so only something equally positive and much denser can turn it around. That is the nucleus.
>
> Electrons cannot do it. They are seven thousand times lighter. A ping-pong ball does not stop a bowling ball.
>
> A sphere with the charge spread evenly would deflect everything a little, and nothing a lot.


### q04 · Why sodium and potassium behave alike

`q04.mp4` · ~39 s · 94 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
Na  2 | 8 | 1        11 protons
K   2 | 8 | 8 | 1    19 protons

same OUTER shell  ->  same chemistry
group = column = family
```

**Pronunciation:** *sodium* → SOH-dee-um · *potassium* → puh-TASS-ee-um · *valence* → VAY-lens

**Say**

> Sodium has eleven electrons. Potassium has nineteen. Completely different numbers, so why do they behave the same?
>
> Look at the outer shell. Both have exactly one electron there.
>
> Chemistry happens in the outer shell. Both atoms do the same thing: they give that one electron away and become a singly charged positive ion. That is why both react violently with water.
>
> Different protons, different mass, same valence electron, same chemistry.
>
> That is what a group in the periodic table is. A column of elements that share the outer shell, and therefore share their behaviour.


## C3 — Chemical bonding

### q05 · Why molten salt conducts

`q05.mp4` · ~40 s · 97 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
NaCl is built from Na+ and Cl- ions

SOLID:  ions locked in the lattice  ->  no current
MELT :  ions can move               ->  current

melting does NOT make electrons
```

**And draw this**

1. On the left draw a 3 by 3 grid of alternating + and - signs. Write "solid" above it.
2. On the right draw the same + and - signs, but scattered, and put a small arrow next to each one. Write "melt" above it.

**Pronunciation:** *lattice* → LAT-iss · *chloride* → KLOR-ide · *ions* → EYE-onz

**Say**

> A current needs charge carriers that can move. Both halves of that sentence matter.
>
> In sodium chloride the charge carriers are already there. The salt is built from sodium ions and chloride ions. That is what makes it a salt.
>
> But in the solid crystal every ion is locked into its place in the lattice. Nothing can flow.
>
> Melting does not create the ions, and it does not create electrons. It only breaks the lattice apart so the ions can drift. Dissolving it does the same.
>
> Ions present but frozen: no current. Ions free to move: current.


### q06 · Why nitrogen forms exactly NH₃

`q06.mp4` · ~40 s · 96 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
N is in group 5   ->  5 valence electrons
octet rule        ->  wants 8
8 - 5 = 3         ->  3 bonds + 1 lone pair

H always makes exactly 1 bond
```

**And draw this**

1. Write a big N in the middle of the sheet.
2. Draw three short lines out of the N: one left, one right, one down. Put an H at the end of each line.
3. Draw two dots on top of the N and write "lone pair" next to them.

**Pronunciation:** *ammonia* → uh-MOH-nee-uh · *octet* → ok-TET · *pyramidal* → pi-RAM-i-dul · *valence* → VAY-lens

**Say**

> Nitrogen sits in group five, so it brings five valence electrons. The octet rule says it wants eight.
>
> Eight minus five is three. So nitrogen needs three more shared electrons, which means three bonds. The two it did not use stay behind as a lone pair.
>
> That gives you ammonia: three nitrogen-hydrogen bonds and one lone pair on top. It also explains why the molecule is pyramidal, not flat.
>
> Hydrogen always forms exactly one bond, because it has one electron and needs two.
>
> Count the valence electrons, fill up to eight, and the formula falls out.


## C4 — Amount of substance

### q07 · The mass of 500 litres of ammonia

`q07.mp4` · ~40 s · 96 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
n = V / Vm  =  500 / 22.4  =  22.3 mol
M(NH3)      =  14.0 + 3 x 1.0  =  17.0 g/mol
m = n x M   =  22.3 x 17.0  =  380 g

L : (L/mol) = mol      mol x g/mol = g
```

**Pronunciation:** *ammonia* → uh-MOH-nee-uh · *molar* → MOH-lar

**Say**

> Never jump straight from litres to grams. Go through moles.
>
> At standard conditions one mole of any gas takes twenty-two point four litres. So five hundred divided by twenty-two point four gives twenty-two point three moles.
>
> Check the units. Litres divided by litres per mole leaves moles. Good.
>
> Now the molar mass. Ammonia is nitrogen, fourteen, plus three times hydrogen. Seventeen grams per mole.
>
> Twenty-two point three times seventeen is about three hundred and eighty grams.
>
> If you got eight and a half thousand, you multiplied five hundred by seventeen and skipped the molar volume completely.


### q08 · Concentration of a sodium hydroxide solution

`q08.mp4` · ~39 s · 95 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
n = m / M  =  4.0 / 40.0  =  0.10 mol
V          =  500 mL  =  0.500 L
c = n / V  =  0.10 / 0.500  =  0.20 mol/L

half the volume  ->  double the concentration
```

**Pronunciation:** *hydroxide* → hy-DROK-side · *concentration* → kon-sen-TRAY-shun

**Say**

> Concentration is moles per litre, so both parts of that need converting first.
>
> The mass. Four grams divided by forty grams per mole gives nought point one moles.
>
> The volume. Five hundred millilitres is nought point five litres. That decimal point is where most of the marks get lost.
>
> Now divide. Nought point one divided by nought point five is nought point two moles per litre.
>
> Sanity check: the same amount of substance in a full litre would give nought point one. Half the volume means double the concentration. So nought point two is right.


## C5 — Chemical reactions

### q09 · The oxyhydrogen reaction

`q09.mp4` · ~41 s · 98 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
2 H2  +  O2  ->  2 H2O

n(H2) = 16.8 / 22.4 = 0.750 mol
n(O2) =  8.4 / 22.4 = 0.375 mol   -> exactly 2 : 1

n(H2O) = 0.750 mol
m = 0.750 x 18.0 = 13.5 g
check: 1.5 g + 12.0 g = 13.5 g
```

**Pronunciation:** *hydrogen* → HY-druh-jen · *oxygen* → OK-si-jen

**Say**

> Balance first. Two hydrogen plus one oxygen gives two water.
>
> Convert both volumes into moles. Sixteen point eight over twenty-two point four is nought point seven five moles of hydrogen. Eight point four gives nought point three seven five moles of oxygen.
>
> That is exactly the two to one ratio the equation asks for, so nothing is left over.
>
> The equation gives as many water molecules as hydrogen molecules. So nought point seven five moles, times eighteen, is thirteen and a half grams.
>
> Mass check: one and a half grams plus twelve grams is thirteen and a half.


### q10 · What a catalyst really does

`q10.mp4` · ~41 s · 99 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
catalyst  ->  LOWERS the activation energy
catalyst  ->  comes out UNCHANGED

it does NOT change the energy released
it does NOT turn endothermic into exothermic
```

**And draw this**

1. Draw an L: vertical line on the left, horizontal line along the bottom. Write "energy" next to the vertical one.
2. Start a line on the left at half height and write "reactants" at that start.
3. Take the line up over a tall hump, then down to an end lower than the start. Write "products" at that end.
4. Draw a second line between the same two ends with a much lower hump. Write "with catalyst" on it.
5. Write underneath: "both ends stay at the same height".

**Pronunciation:** *catalyst* → KAT-uh-list · *activation* → ak-ti-VAY-shun · *endothermic* → en-doh-THER-mik

**Say**

> Draw the energy diagram. Reactants on the left, products on the right, and a hill in between. That hill is the activation energy.
>
> A catalyst does one thing. It lowers that hill by offering a different route. More particles now have enough energy to get over it, so the reaction runs faster.
>
> What it does not do is move the left end or the right end of the diagram. The energy released stays exactly the same, and an endothermic reaction stays endothermic.
>
> And the catalyst comes out unchanged. That is why a tiny amount can convert tonnes of material.


## C6 — Intermolecular forces

### q11 · Why methanol boils higher than chloromethane

`q11.mp4` · ~40 s · 96 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
CH3Cl   50.5 g/mol   boils at -23.8 °C
CH4O    32.0 g/mol   boils at +65 °C

boiling does NOT break bonds inside the molecule

CH3Cl : dipole-dipole
CH4O  : HYDROGEN BONDS (O-H)  -> much stronger
```

**Pronunciation:** *chloromethane* → klor-oh-METH-ayn · *methanol* → METH-uh-nol · *dipole* → DY-pole

**Say**

> The big misconception first. Boiling does not break the bonds inside a molecule. The methanol that evaporates is still C H four O.
>
> Boiling only pulls molecules away from each other. So the only thing that matters is the force between them.
>
> Chloromethane is a dipole, because the carbon-chlorine bond is polar. That gives dipole-dipole forces.
>
> Methanol has an O H group, hydrogen sitting directly on oxygen. That means hydrogen bonds, the strongest of the intermolecular forces.
>
> Those cost far more energy to overcome, so methanol boils almost ninety degrees higher, even though it is lighter.


### q12 · Why non-polar chlorine can be liquefied

`q12.mp4` · ~40 s · 96 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
Cl-Cl : two identical atoms  ->  no permanent dipole
no hydrogen  ->  no hydrogen bonds
no ions

electrons shift for a moment  ->  temporary dipole
that induces a dipole in the neighbour
= VAN DER WAALS force
```

**And draw this**

1. Draw two circles touching each other. Write Cl in each. That is one molecule.
2. Shade the right circle a bit darker. Write a minus sign on the right and a plus sign on the left.
3. Draw a second identical pair to the right of it, shaded the same way.
4. Draw a small arrow between the two pairs and write "attraction".

**Pronunciation:** *van der Waals* → van der VAALS · *helium* → HEE-lee-um · *dipole* → DY-pole

**Say**

> Chlorine is two identical atoms, so the electronegativity difference is zero. No permanent dipole. There is no hydrogen, so no hydrogen bonds. And there are no ions.
>
> So what holds it together at minus thirty-five degrees?
>
> The electrons are moving all the time. For a split second they sit more on one side, and the molecule becomes a temporary dipole. That induces a dipole in its neighbour too.
>
> That is a van der Waals force. Weak, short-lived, but present between all particles.
>
> It is why even helium can be liquefied if you cool it far enough.


## C7 — Acids & bases

### q13 · pH 3 against pH 6

`q13.mp4` · ~39 s · 94 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
pH 3  ->  c(H3O+) = 10^-3 mol/L
pH 6  ->  c(H3O+) = 10^-6 mol/L

10^-3 / 10^-6 = 10^3 = 1000 times

1 pH unit = factor 10
LOWER pH = MORE acidic
```

**Pronunciation:** *oxonium* → ok-SOH-nee-um · *logarithmic* → log-uh-RITH-mik

**Say**

> The pH scale is logarithmic, and that is the whole question.
>
> pH three means the oxonium concentration is ten to the minus three moles per litre. pH six means ten to the minus six.
>
> Divide them and you get ten to the power of three. A factor of one thousand.
>
> Subtracting six minus three gives you the number of steps, not the factor. Each of those three steps is itself a factor of ten.
>
> And watch the direction. A lower pH means more acidic, so a higher concentration.
>
> One pH unit, factor ten. Always.


### q14 · The neutralisation volume

`q14.mp4` · ~41 s · 98 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
NaOH  +  HCl  ->  NaCl  +  H2O      (1 : 1)

n(NaOH) = 0.0250 L x 0.100 mol/L = 2.50 mmol
n(HCl)  = 2.50 mmol
V(HCl)  = 2.50 mmol / 0.200 mol/L = 12.5 mL

twice as concentrated -> half the volume
```

**Pronunciation:** *hydrochloric* → hy-druh-KLOR-ik · *hydroxide* → hy-DROK-side · *millimoles* → MILL-i-molz

**Say**

> Never compare volumes directly. Compare amounts of substance.
>
> Start with the base. Nought point nought two five litres times nought point one moles per litre gives two point five millimoles.
>
> The equation is one to one, so you need exactly two point five millimoles of hydrochloric acid.
>
> Now turn that back into a volume. Two point five millimoles divided by nought point two moles per litre is twelve point five millilitres.
>
> And it makes sense. The acid is twice as concentrated, so you need half the volume.
>
> Two clean steps, and always check whether the answer is plausible.


## C8 — Redox reactions

### q15 · Oxidation number of chromium in dichromate

`q15.mp4` · ~40 s · 97 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
Cr2O7 2-

O is -II   ->  7 x (-2) = -14
total charge of the ion = -2

2x - 14 = -2
2x = +12
x  = +VI     (per chromium atom)
```

**Pronunciation:** *chromium* → KROH-mee-um · *dichromate* → dy-KROH-mate · *permanganate* → per-MANG-guh-nate

**Say**

> Set up the little equation and this is quick.
>
> Oxygen is almost always minus two, and there are seven of them. Seven times minus two is minus fourteen.
>
> The whole ion carries a charge of minus two, and all the oxidation numbers have to add up to that charge.
>
> So: two x, minus fourteen, equals minus two. Two x is plus twelve. And x is plus six.
>
> Two traps. Forgetting to divide by the two chromium atoms gives plus twelve. And plus seven is manganese in permanganate, a different ion.
>
> Count the atoms, write the equation, divide.


### q16 · Zinc, copper and the electrochemical series

`q16.mp4` · ~40 s · 97 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
Zn  +  Cu2+   ->   Zn2+  +  Cu      RUNS
Cu  +  Zn2+   ->   nothing           DOES NOT RUN

Zn = less noble = gives electrons away
Cu = more noble = keeps its electrons

seen: red-brown layer on the zinc, blue colour fades
```

**Pronunciation:** *sulfate* → SUL-fayt · *noble* → NOH-bul · *deposited* → di-POZ-it-id

**Say**

> Only one of these two beakers does anything.
>
> Zinc is the less noble metal. It stands higher in the electrochemical series, which means it gives up its electrons more readily.
>
> So in the copper sulfate beaker, zinc hands two electrons to each copper ion. The zinc dissolves as Zn two plus, copper is deposited as a red-brown layer on the strip, and the blue colour fades.
>
> In the other beaker, nothing happens. Copper is more noble and will not give its electrons to zinc ions.
>
> The series tells you the direction, and it only runs one way.


## C9 — Organic chemistry

### q17 · Butane against 2-methylpropane

`q17.mp4` · ~40 s · 96 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
both are C4H10  ->  both 58 g/mol
no O, no N      ->  no hydrogen bonds

butane          C-C-C-C        straight, long
2-methylpropane branched       compact

less contact area -> weaker van der Waals -> lower bp
```

**And draw this**

1. Write four C in a row joined by short lines: C-C-C-C. Write "butane" under it.
2. Below that write three C in a row joined by lines, then one more C above the middle C, joined by a short line. Write "2-methylpropane" under it.
3. Next to both write "C4H10, 58 g/mol".

**Pronunciation:** *butane* → BYOO-tayn · *2-methylpropane* → two meth-ill-PROH-payn · *van der Waals* → van der VAALS · *isomers* → EYE-so-merz

**Say**

> Same molecular formula, C four H ten. So the same molar mass. The mass cannot be the answer.
>
> No oxygen and no nitrogen, so no hydrogen bonds either.
>
> What actually differs is the shape.
>
> Butane is a straight chain. The molecules lie against each other like pencils in a box, touching over a large surface, so there are many van der Waals contacts.
>
> 2-methylpropane is branched and compact, more like a ball. Neighbours touch over a smaller area, so fewer contacts and weaker attraction.
>
> Weaker attraction means a lower boiling point. More branching, lower boiling point.


### q18 · How the body breaks ethanol down

`q18.mp4` · ~40 s · 97 words

**Write this on the board** (copy it exactly)

```
ethanol  ->  ethanal  ->  ethanoic acid
alcohol  ->  aldehyde ->  carboxylic acid

step 1: enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase
step 2: oxidised further to the acid

the carbon chain never gets shorter
ethanal = the hangover
```

**Pronunciation:** *ethanol* → ETH-uh-nol · *ethanal* → ETH-uh-nal · *acetaldehyde* → ass-uh-TAL-duh-hide · *ethanoic* → eth-uh-NOH-ik · *aldehyde* → AL-duh-hide · *carboxylic* → kar-bok-SIL-ik · *dehydrogenase* → dee-hy-DROJ-uh-nayz

**Say**

> Ethanol is a primary alcohol: the O H group sits on a carbon that still carries hydrogen. Oxidation then always runs in two steps.
>
> Step one. The enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase removes hydrogen, and you get the aldehyde, ethanal. You may know it as acetaldehyde.
>
> Step two. Ethanal is oxidised further to the carboxylic acid, ethanoic acid, which the body then breaks down completely.
>
> Notice that the carbon chain never gets shorter. Oxidation changes the functional group, not the skeleton.
>
> And ethanal is the important one. It is far more toxic than ethanol, and it causes the hangover.
